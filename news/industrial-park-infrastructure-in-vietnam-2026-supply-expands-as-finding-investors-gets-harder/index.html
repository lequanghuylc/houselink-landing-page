Industrial Park Infrastructure in Vietnam 2026: Supply Expands as Finding Investors Gets Harder

FDI into Vietnam remains strong, yet for many industrial parks the race for tenants has never been tougher. Resolution 10-NQ/TW sets a new direction, and with it a need to rethink how investment promotion is done.

HOUSELINK Oct 13, 2026

FDI into Vietnam remains strong, yet for many industrial parks the race for tenants has never been tougher. Resolution 10-NQ/TW sets a new direction, and with it a need to rethink how investment promotion is done.

The capital keeps coming

According to the National Statistics Office, registered foreign investment into Vietnam reached USD 50.36 billion by 30 September 2026, up 76.4% year on year, while disbursed FDI reached an estimated USD 21.07 billion, the highest nine-month figure in five years. At the macro level, Vietnam remains an attractive destination.

But supply is growing faster

HOUSELINK's industrial park database (updated August 2026) covers more than 500 industrial parks and clusters with more than 149,000 hectares of planned area, including about 35,300 hectares of vacant industrial land and 207 zones with more than 20 hectares still available. Even so, new supply keeps arriving at speed. According to data compiled by HOUSELINK, at least 26 new industrial parks broke ground nationwide in the first nine months of 2026, covering close to 6,000 hectares, along with at least 12 industrial clusters. Quang Ninh alone started 7 parks on 27 August, Hai Phong 4 on 12 May and Bac Ninh 4 during the year. Northern industrial land passed 25,200 hectares by Q2 2026, up 15.7% year on year, with roughly 7,200 more hectares expected between 2026 and 2028. With supply running ahead of demand, northern occupancy has slipped to about 64%, 2.7 percentage points lower than a year earlier, even though leasing demand has recovered.

When parks look alike

Volume is only part of the problem. Most parks are planned as multi-sector; those with a focus tend to pick the same industries: high-tech, electronics, semiconductors. Domestic supporting industries are not yet strong enough to set parks apart along the value chain. To an investor, many brochures could almost swap names.

The result is that competition slides towards rent and incentives. Promotion costs rise, prices come under pressure, and returns fall.

Promotion has barely changed

While the market has shifted, the way parks find investors has stayed much the same for more than a decade: in-house sales teams, broker networks, event organisers and the direction of local authorities. These channels still have value, but they share a weakness. Information usually arrives after the investor has narrowed the options, and it arrives at many parks at once. The cost of each investor who actually signs keeps rising.

Level 3 infrastructure and a platform mindset

Most parks still offer basic infrastructure: serviced land, roads, power, water and wastewater treatment, which HOUSELINK classes as levels 1 and 2. Multinationals, meanwhile, increasingly ask about renewable power, green certification, circularity and emissions data to meet their ESG commitments and schemes such as the EU's CBAM. That is level 3 infrastructure. According to the forum on eco-industrial and smart parks, only about 1-2% of parks are converting to the eco-industrial model. To help close that gap, HOUSELINK and its partners are delivering ESG infrastructure advisory for industrial parks, under MoUs signed with BECAMEX VSIP Binh Dinh and GLASSDOME (March 2026), a tripartite MoU with KBC and GLASSDOME (June 2026) and a tripartite MoU with Da Hop and GLASSDOME for Tan Vien Tech Valley Industrial Park (August 2026).

The biggest difference lies in mindset: treating a park as a real estate project to subdivide and lease, or as an infrastructure platform supporting sustainable production for decades.

Resolution 10 changes the rules

Resolution 10-NQ/TW of 8 June 2026 on developing the foreign-invested economy targets USD 200-300 billion of registered FDI for 2026-2030 and shifts the approach from attracting investment by administrative area to attracting it by industry cluster and value chain. It prioritises high technology, core technology, the green and digital economy and stronger links with domestic firms, targets about 10% of parks as eco-industrial parks, and calls for a new approach to investment promotion. It also states plainly that some localities still compete for investment on volume.

What it means for industrial parks

When policy favours quality, every park needs clearer answers to two questions. Which investors truly fit our infrastructure, industries and energy supply? And how do we meet them before they finalise their shortlist? With those answers, a park can stop spreading its resources thin, focus on the few investors most likely to choose it, and negotiate on value rather than price.
